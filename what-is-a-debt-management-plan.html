What Is a Debt Management Plan? How It Works and Who It Helps

How a nonprofit debt management plan works, what it costs, the trade-offs, who it suits, and how to choose a trustworthy credit counselor.

October 18, 2026 · 3 min read

This article is educational and not financial advice. BizMasterPro may be compensated when you call providers we feature.

A debt management plan, or DMP, is a repayment plan set up through a credit counseling organization. Instead of paying each credit card separately, you make one monthly deposit, and the counselor pays your creditors for you. It's different from debt settlement: you repay what you owe, often with lower interest or fees, rather than negotiating the balance down.

This is educational information, not financial advice. A certified credit counselor can tell you whether a plan fits your situation.

How a debt management plan works

According to the FTC, a DMP generally works like this:

  1. A counselor reviews your finances. That means income, expenses and each debt you owe.
  2. The counselor sets up a payment schedule with you and your creditors. Creditors may agree to lower your interest rates or waive certain fees.
  3. You make one deposit each month with the credit counseling organization.
  4. The counselor pays your creditors from those deposits, following the plan.

DMPs cover unsecured debts such as credit cards, medical bills and some student loans. They aren't for debts secured by collateral, like a mortgage or car loan (FTC).

The trade-offs

A DMP can make payments simpler and cheaper, but it's a real commitment.

  • It takes years. The FTC says a plan "can take 48 months or more to complete."
  • Every payment has to be on time. Missing deposits can put the plan, and any interest concessions, at risk.
  • Your credit use is limited. You might have to agree not to apply for or use new credit until the plan is finished. Some plans close enrolled card accounts, which can affect your credit, so ask how your accounts will be handled.
  • There may be fees. Many organizations charge low setup and monthly fees, but "nonprofit" doesn't automatically mean free (FTC).
  • Not every creditor has to agree. Check with each creditor that it offers the changes the counselor describes.

How it compares

Debt management plan Debt settlement
What you repay The full balance, often with reduced interest or fees A negotiated amount less than the balance
Who runs it Credit counseling organization, often nonprofit For-profit settlement company
Typical timeline 48 months or more Often 2–4 years
Payments to creditors Continue each month through the plan Usually stop while you save
Main risks Missed deposits, fees, limited credit use Credit damage, collection lawsuits, fees, taxes on forgiven debt

Because you repay the full balance in a DMP, you generally don't have forgiven principal to report as canceled debt. Settlement often does; see our guide to taxes on settled debt.

Who a DMP tends to help

A DMP may fit if you:

  • Have mostly credit card or other unsecured debt
  • Have steady income that covers your basic expenses plus one consolidated payment
  • Are struggling mainly because of high interest rates
  • Want to repay what you owe and can commit to several years

It may not fit if your income can't cover even a reduced payment, if most of your debt is secured, or if you're facing lawsuits or wage garnishment. In those cases, a counselor may discuss other options, including bankruptcy. The FTC warns that if a counselor says a DMP is your only option, especially without reviewing your finances in detail, you should find a different counselor.

How to choose a credit counselor

The FTC suggests interviewing a few counselors and asking (FTC):

  • What will you do to help me?
  • How much will I have to pay?
  • Do you have free education and information?
  • Are you licensed to work here?

Choose an organization that:

  • Doesn't charge in advance for help it hasn't given yet
  • Has counselors accredited or certified by an outside organization
  • Offers budget counseling and free educational materials, not just DMPs
  • Gives you a written quote for any one-time or monthly fees
  • Will help you even if you can't afford the fees

Check any organization with your state attorney general and local consumer protection agency, and get every promise in writing.

Where to start

The National Foundation for Credit Counseling (NFCC) is a network of nonprofit agencies with certified counselors, and an initial counseling session is a low-pressure way to see all your options.
