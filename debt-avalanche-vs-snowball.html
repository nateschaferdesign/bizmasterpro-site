Debt Avalanche vs. Debt Snowball: Which Payoff Method Fits You?

How the avalanche and snowball debt payoff methods work, a worked example of the cost difference, what research says about sticking with each, and how to choose.

October 28, 2026 · 3 min read

This article is educational and not financial advice. BizMasterPro may be compensated when you call providers we feature.

Short answer: the avalanche method (highest interest rate first) saves the most money. The snowball method (smallest balance first) gets you quicker wins, which research suggests helps many people keep going. Both work if you stick with them. The CFPB describes both and says the right choice depends on what keeps you on track.

This is educational information, not financial advice.

How both methods start

Both methods follow the same basic rules:

  1. List every debt with its balance, interest rate and minimum payment.
  2. Pay the minimum on every debt, every month.
  3. Put any extra money toward one target debt.
  4. When the target is paid off, roll its payment into the next target.

The only difference is how you pick the target.

The avalanche method: highest rate first

Order your debts from the highest interest rate to the lowest. Extra money goes to the highest-rate debt first.

  • Pro: You pay the least total interest. The CFPB says paying the highest-interest debts first can save money in the long run.
  • Con: If your highest-rate debt has a big balance, it may take a long time to pay off your first account.

The snowball method: smallest balance first

Order your debts from the smallest balance to the largest, regardless of rate. Extra money goes to the smallest one first.

  • Pro: You see progress quickly as small debts disappear.
  • Con: The CFPB notes you "may end up paying more in the long run," because larger, costlier debts keep growing while you pay off the small ones (CFPB).

A worked example

This is a hypothetical example: three debts, $200 a month extra on top of the minimums ($650 a month in total), and no new charges.

Debt Balance Interest rate Minimum
Medical payment plan $1,200 0% $50
Personal loan $5,000 12% $150
Credit card $9,000 25% $250
Avalanche Snowball
Order Credit card → loan → medical Medical → loan → credit card
First debt paid off Month 24 Month 5
Debt-free Month 30 Month 31
Total interest About $3,750 About $4,750

In this example, the avalanche saves about $1,000 and finishes a month sooner. But with the snowball, the first debt is gone in five months instead of two years. Your numbers will differ, so run your own with a payoff calculator.

What research says about sticking with it

Researchers at Northwestern's Kellogg School studied about 6,000 debt-settlement clients and found that the number of accounts people fully paid off was a better predictor of becoming debt-free than the dollar amount paid (Wikipedia summary of Gal & McShane, 2012). A later study in Harvard Business Review reached a similar conclusion: "focusing on paying down the account with the smallest balance tends to have the most powerful effect on people's sense of progress" (CNBC).

In other words, the cheapest plan on paper only helps if you finish it.

How to choose

Pick the avalanche if... Pick the snowball if...
Your rates differ a lot Your rates are fairly similar
You're motivated by saving money You're motivated by quick wins
You can stay on track without early payoffs You've started payoff plans before and stopped

A middle path: pay off one small debt first for a quick win, then switch to the avalanche for the rest.

Make either method work better

  • Automate minimum payments so you never miss one.
  • Stop adding new charges to the debts you're paying off.
  • Ask about lower rates. A balance transfer or consolidation loan might lower your highest rates.
  • Put windfalls toward your target debt, such as tax refunds or bonuses.

If the numbers don't work

If your minimum payments alone take up more than you can afford, a payoff method may not be enough. A nonprofit credit counselor through the NFCC can review your budget and explain options like a debt management plan. Debt settlement is another option, but it comes with credit damage, possible lawsuits, fees, possible taxes on forgiven debt, and programs that often take 2–4 years.
