What Happens to Your Credit Score During Debt Settlement

Why scores usually drop during debt settlement, how long the marks stay on your report, and what recovery can look like afterward.

October 12, 2026 · 3 min read

This article is educational and not financial advice. BizMasterPro may be compensated when you call providers we feature.

The short answer: debt settlement usually hurts your credit, and the damage starts well before any debt is settled. The good news is that the impact fades over time, and there are concrete steps that help you rebuild. Here's what happens at each stage and why.

Why scores drop: payment history

Payment history is the biggest single factor in FICO scores, making up 35% of the score, according to myFICO. Most settlement programs work by having you stop paying creditors while you save, which the CFPB says can hurt your credit scores and your ability to get credit in the future.

So the biggest hit usually comes from the missed payments themselves, not from the settlement at the end.

What happens at each stage

Stage What gets reported Likely effect
You stop paying Late payments, 30, 60, 90+ days Scores typically fall, often sharply
Months of non-payment The account may be charged off or sent to collections More negative marks
The debt settles Status updated to show it was settled for less than the full balance Still negative, though the balance owed drops
After the program Negative marks age Their impact lessens over time

A settled account is commonly reported as "settled for less than full balance," and that notation is a negative mark (Bills.com). It tells future lenders the original agreement wasn't paid as agreed.

How long it stays on your report

Credit bureaus can generally report most negative information for seven years (CFPB). For collection accounts, myFICO says the seven years run from the original delinquency date, meaning the first missed payment after which the account was never brought current (myFICO).

That detail matters: the clock usually starts when you first fall behind, not when the settlement is finished. If you settle two years into a program, the negative mark may have about five years left.

It fades before it disappears

myFICO notes that "the older a credit problem, the less it counts toward your credit score," and that paying on time afterward gives your scores more room to rise. A two-year-old settlement generally weighs less than a fresh one, even while it's still on your report.

What it means in real life

Plan for a lower score during the program and for some time after. That can mean:

  • Higher interest rates or denials on new credit cards and loans
  • A harder time qualifying for a mortgage or car loan
  • Extra scrutiny from some landlords

If you expect to need any of these in the next few years, factor that into your decision.

How to rebuild afterward

  1. Check your reports. Make sure every settled account shows the correct status and a zero balance. You can get free reports at AnnualCreditReport.com.
  2. Dispute errors. If a settled debt still shows a balance, dispute it with the credit bureau and keep your settlement letters.
  3. Pay every remaining bill on time. New on-time history is the strongest way to rebuild.
  4. Keep balances low. Low balances on any cards you still have help your scores.
  5. Consider a secured card if you have no open credit, and pay it in full each month.

Is there a gentler option?

A nonprofit debt management plan usually keeps your accounts current, since you keep making one monthly payment, so it's often easier on your credit than settlement. You can find a counselor through the NFCC. Our three-option comparison and cost breakdown can help you weigh the trade-offs.
