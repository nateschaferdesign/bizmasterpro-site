Balance Transfer Card vs. Debt Consolidation Loan: Which Fits You?

How balance transfer cards and debt consolidation loans work, what they really cost, the fine print to watch for, and how to choose between them.

October 22, 2026 · 3 min read

This article is educational and not financial advice. BizMasterPro may be compensated when you call providers we feature.

If you're paying high interest on several credit cards, combining them into one lower-rate payment can save money and simplify your month. The two most common ways to do it are a balance transfer credit card and a debt consolidation loan. Both can help, but they work differently and have different fine print. Here's how to compare them, using guidance from the CFPB and the FTC.

This is educational information, not financial advice.

How a balance transfer card works

You open a new credit card, or use an existing one, and move your balances onto it. Many issuers offer a 0% or low promotional rate on transferred balances for a limited time (CFPB).

What to know:

  • There's usually a fee. It's typically a percentage of the amount transferred or a flat amount, whichever is more. The CFPB notes some issuers charge 3 to 5 percent (CFPB). A 0% offer can still have a fee (CFPB).
  • The low rate ends. Introductory rates must last at least six months, and the issuer must tell you the rate that applies afterward (CFPB).
  • Late payments are costly. If you're more than 60 days late, the issuer can raise the rate on all balances, including the transferred balance.
  • New purchases can cost interest. If you use the same card for purchases, you generally lose the interest-free grace period on them until the entire balance is paid off.

How a debt consolidation loan works

You borrow a lump sum from a bank, credit union or other lender, use it to pay off your cards, and then repay the loan in fixed monthly payments (FTC).

What to know:

  • Your rate depends on your credit. The loan only saves money if its rate, plus fees, is lower than what you pay now.
  • There may be upfront costs. The FTC notes some loans charge "points," with one point equal to 1% of the amount you borrow.
  • Longer terms can cost more. A lower monthly payment spread over more years can mean paying more interest overall. Compare the total cost, not just the payment.
  • Be careful with your home. Some consolidation loans, like home equity loans, use your home as collateral. The FTC warns that if you can't make the payments, you could lose your home.

Side by side

Balance transfer card Debt consolidation loan
Interest Promotional rate for a set time, then a higher rate Fixed or variable rate for the full term
Upfront cost Balance transfer fee, often a percentage Possible origination fees or points
Payment Minimum payment changes with the balance Fixed monthly payment
Timeline Best if you can pay it off before the promo ends Set term, often several years
Biggest risk The rate jumps after the promo or after late payments Paying more overall with a long term, or risking collateral
Best for Smaller balances you can clear quickly Larger balances that need a fixed payoff schedule

A simple way to decide

  1. Add up your balances and current rates.
  2. Estimate what you can pay each month. Be realistic.
  3. Check the balance transfer math. Divide your balance by the number of promotional months. If you can pay that much each month, a transfer may cost less overall, even with the fee.
  4. Check the loan math. Compare total repayment, including fees, with what you'd pay by staying on your current cards.
  5. Stop adding new card debt. Consolidation only works if the old cards don't fill back up.

When you move a balance, the CFPB suggests not closing your old account right away and making at least the minimum payment until the transfer goes through.

If neither fits

If your credit makes these options too expensive, or the monthly payment is still out of reach, other paths include a nonprofit debt management plan or debt settlement. Settlement comes with serious trade-offs: credit damage, possible lawsuits, fees, possible taxes on forgiven debt, and programs that often take 2–4 years.
