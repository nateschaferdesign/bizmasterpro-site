How Much Debt Do You Need for Debt Relief?

Most debt settlement companies require $7,500 to $10,000 in unsecured debt. Here's why minimums exist, what counts, and what to consider if you owe less.

October 24, 2026 · 3 min read

This article is educational and not financial advice. BizMasterPro may be compensated when you call providers we feature.

If you're looking into debt relief, one of the first questions is whether you owe enough to qualify. For debt settlement, the answer is usually yes if you have at least $7,500 to $10,000 in unsecured debt. Other options, like a nonprofit debt management plan or a consolidation loan, work differently. Here's what to know.

This is educational information, not financial advice.

Typical minimums for debt settlement

Most debt settlement companies set a minimum enrolled balance, usually $7,500 or $10,000 (CNBC Select). Two large companies, from their own websites:

Company Minimum stated on its site
Freedom Debt Relief $7,500 in unsecured debt (Freedom Debt Relief)
National Debt Relief $10,000 in unsecured debt (National Debt Relief)

Some third-party reviews list different figures, and requirements can vary by state and situation, so confirm directly before you enroll.

Why minimums exist

Settlement companies are typically paid a percentage of the debt they settle, so a small balance may not cover their costs. A smaller balance also gives creditors less reason to accept a reduced payoff (CNBC Select).

What counts toward the minimum

Settlement generally covers unsecured debt, meaning debt without collateral:

  • Credit cards
  • Medical bills
  • Many personal loans
  • Some private student loans

It generally doesn't cover secured debt like mortgages and auto loans, and settlement companies typically can't negotiate federal student loans (CNBC Select). For federal student loans, repayment and forgiveness programs are free through StudentAid.gov.

Meeting the minimum isn't the same as being a good fit

Even if you qualify, debt settlement comes with real trade-offs:

  • Credit damage. Programs usually involve pausing payments to creditors while you save, which leads to late payments on your credit report.
  • Possible lawsuits. Creditors can still sue while you're enrolled. See our guide on being sued during debt relief.
  • Fees. Settlement fees are often a percentage of enrolled debt, and can only be charged after a debt is settled (FTC).
  • Taxes. Forgiven debt can count as taxable income. See our Form 1099-C guide.
  • Time. Programs often take 2–4 years.

Most companies also expect you to be able to make steady monthly deposits into a dedicated account.

If you owe less than the minimum

Smaller balances often have better options than settlement:

  1. Call your card issuer. Ask about a hardship program, a lower rate or a payment plan.
  2. Consider a balance transfer or consolidation loan if your credit allows it. Our comparison explains both.
  3. Try a payoff method. The avalanche (highest rate first) or snowball (smallest balance first) method can work well for smaller balances.
  4. Talk to a nonprofit credit counselor. They can review your budget and explain whether a debt management plan makes sense.

Where to start

Whatever you owe, a good first step is a session with a nonprofit credit counselor through the NFCC. They can review your full situation and explain every option, including ones that don't involve settlement. Before choosing any company, review the red flags of debt relief scams.
