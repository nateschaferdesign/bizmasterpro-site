Medical Debt: Your Options and Your Rights

How medical debt shows up on credit reports in 2026, how to check your bills, ask for hospital financial assistance, and choose a repayment option.

October 26, 2026 · 3 min read

This article is educational and not financial advice. BizMasterPro may be compensated when you call providers we feature.

Medical bills are different from most debt. People rarely choose them or know the price ahead of time. They're also more likely to contain errors. That's why there are special rules about how medical debt is reported and how nonprofit hospitals must offer help. Here's what applies as of October 2026.

This is educational information, not legal or financial advice.

How medical debt affects your credit report

The three nationwide credit bureaus, Equifax, Experian and TransUnion, voluntarily changed how they report medical collections (Equifax):

Change Effective
Paid medical collection debt no longer appears on credit reports July 1, 2022
Unpaid medical collections don't appear until they're a year old (up from six months) July 1, 2022
Medical collections with an initial balance under $500 removed April 2023

Unpaid medical collections of $500 or more that are over a year old can still appear.

What happened to the federal rule?

In January 2025, the CFPB finalized a rule that would have removed medical debt from credit reports used by lenders. In July 2025, a federal court vacated that rule, so it isn't in effect (CFPB, Congressional Research Service). The bureaus' voluntary changes above still apply.

Check your reports. You can get your free credit reports at AnnualCreditReport.com. If you see a paid medical collection, one under $500, or one less than a year old, you can dispute it with the bureau.

Step 1: Make sure the bill is right

Before paying anything:

  1. Ask for an itemized bill listing every service and charge.
  2. Compare it with your insurer's explanation of benefits to see what was covered and what you owe.
  3. Look for errors like duplicate charges, services you didn't get, or claims that were never sent to insurance.
  4. Call your insurer if a claim was denied, and ask how to appeal.

Step 2: Ask about financial assistance

Tax-exempt nonprofit hospitals must have a written financial assistance policy under federal tax law. According to the IRS, that policy must:

  • Cover all emergency and other medically necessary care
  • Explain who qualifies and whether help means free or discounted care
  • Explain how to apply
  • Be widely publicized, including on the hospital's website and in a notice on billing statements

Eligibility rules vary by hospital, and many cover people well above the poverty line. Ask for the application even if you've already received a bill, and even if the account has gone to collections.

Step 3: Negotiate or set up a payment plan

  • Ask for a discount for paying a lump sum, or if you're uninsured.
  • Ask for an interest-free payment plan directly with the provider. Many offer them.
  • Be cautious with medical credit cards and loans offered at the front desk. Promotional rates can end, and deferred interest can apply to the full original balance.
  • Get any agreement in writing before you pay.

Step 4: If the debt is in collections

  • You can ask the collector to verify the debt. Ask in writing for the creditor's name and the amount.
  • Check whether it's reportable. Paid, under-$500 or less-than-a-year-old medical collections shouldn't appear on your report.
  • Don't ignore lawsuits. If you're served, respond by the deadline. See our guide on what to do if you're sued.
  • Get any settlement in writing stating that your payment resolves the debt.

Bigger options, and their trade-offs

If medical bills are part of a larger debt problem:

  • Nonprofit credit counseling. A counselor can review your budget and explain whether a debt management plan fits. Find one through the NFCC.
  • Debt settlement. Medical debt is unsecured, so it can be enrolled. But settlement comes with credit damage, possible lawsuits, fees, possible taxes on forgiven debt, and programs that often take 2–4 years. See our guide on how much debt you need for debt relief.
  • Bankruptcy. For overwhelming debt, a bankruptcy attorney can explain your options.
