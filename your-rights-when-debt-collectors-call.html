Your Rights When Debt Collectors Call

Federal law limits what debt collectors can do: no calls before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m., no harassment, no lies, a required validation notice and a 30-day window to dispute. Here's what to do when they call.

November 18, 2026 · 4 min read

This article is educational and not financial advice. BizMasterPro may be compensated when you call providers we feature.

Short answer: if a debt collector contacts you, federal law gives you real protections. The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA) makes harassment and deception illegal, limits when and how often collectors can contact you, and requires them to give you information about the debt, including your right to dispute it within 30 days.

Here's what collectors can't do, what they must do, and the steps to take when a call comes in.

What debt collectors can't do

The FDCPA bans harassment, oppression, abuse and deception. The CFPB and the FTC give these examples.

Contact rules

  • No calls before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m., unless you agree otherwise.
  • No calls at work once you tell them you can't take calls there.
  • No calling more than seven times within seven days about a particular debt, and none within seven days after a phone conversation with you about that debt.
  • No contact at an inconvenient time or place they know about.
  • No contact about your debt with most third parties. They can only ask others for your address, home phone number or workplace. Debt amounts stay between you and them.
  • No contact if you tell them in writing to stop. You can also demand they only contact you through your attorney.

Behavior and statements

  • No repeated calls intended to annoy, abuse or harass you.
  • No obscene or profane language, and no threats of violence.
  • No publishing lists of people who don't pay their debts.
  • No lying, including about how much you owe, whether they're an attorney, or whether a lawsuit is planned when they don't mean to file one.
  • No false threats of arrest. Not paying a consumer debt is not a crime, and you can't be jailed for it.
  • No pretending to be a government agency or law enforcement.

If a collector threatens arrest, uses abuse or claims to be police or an attorney when they aren't, that's a red flag. It may be a scam, or an illegal collection. The CFPB advises never to give or confirm bank account, credit card or full Social Security details unless you've confirmed the caller is a real collector.

What debt collectors must do

Identify themselves

Collectors must identify themselves on the phone, including who they are and that they're trying to collect a debt, according to the FTC.

Send validation information

After first contact, the collector must give you "validation information" during or within five days of the first communication, by mail or electronically, per the CFPB's debt collection rule. This includes:

  • the name of the creditor,
  • the amount owed,
  • how to get a breakdown of the amount if you ask for it,
  • your right to dispute the debt, and
  • the end date of the 30-day period in which you can dispute.

Stop collecting if you dispute in writing

If you dispute the debt in writing within the 30-day validation period, the collector must stop collecting the disputed amount until they've sent you verification of the debt or a copy of a judgment, according to the CFPB. Once they've sent verification, collection can continue unless you take further action.

Three steps to take when a collector calls

1. Stay calm and take notes. Write down the date, time, collector's name, company and what was said. Never ignore a collector, but don't agree to anything on the spot, and never pay based on a phone call alone.

2. Verify the debt before paying. Wait for the validation notice. Match it against your own records. If you don't think you owe the debt, or the amount is wrong, send a written dispute within the 30-day window. Keep a copy.

3. Know your three options. You can dispute the debt, tell them to stop contacting you, or negotiate. If you're thinking about settling, see our guide on negotiating with a debt collector, and keep in mind that settled debt usually has credit consequences and canceled amounts can be taxable, per the IRS.

How to make a collector stop contacting you

Send a letter telling them to stop, keep a copy, and send it by certified mail if possible. Once they receive it, they can only contact you to confirm there will be no further contact, or to tell you a specific action, like a lawsuit, per the CFPB.

Two important limits: telling them to stop doesn't make the debt go away, and they can still sue you. Also note that this right doesn't apply to the original creditor in most cases, since the FDCPA covers third-party collectors.

What's outside the FDCPA

A few situations have different rules:

  • The original creditor. The FDCPA generally covers third-party debt collectors, not the original lender collecting its own debt. Many states have their own laws that cover original creditors.
  • Business debts. The FDCPA covers personal, family and household debts, not business debts.
  • Debts owed to a bank may differ from debts owed to a hospital or a payday lender, and collectors sometimes switch between methods, such as calls, texts and email.

How to report a violation

If a collector breaks the rules, you can:

  1. Submit a complaint with the CFPB or call 855-411-CFPB.
  2. Contact your state attorney general, many of which enforce their own collection laws.
  3. Sue the collector. The CFPB notes that FDCPA lawsuits must generally be filed within one year of the violation, so don't delay. A consumer protection attorney can tell you whether your case qualifies.

The bottom line

You have the right to be treated respectfully, to see the details of the debt in writing, and to dispute it. Don't pay anything until the validation notice arrives and matches your records. If you're behind on bills generally, a nonprofit credit counselor affiliated with the National Foundation for Credit Counseling can review your whole situation and options, including a debt management plan.
