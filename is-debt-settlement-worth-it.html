Is Debt Settlement Worth It? The Real Costs, Added Up

Fees, growing balances, credit damage, lawsuits and taxes: a worked example of what debt settlement can really cost, and when it might still make sense.

October 10, 2026 · 3 min read

This article is educational and not financial advice. BizMasterPro may be compensated when you call providers we feature.

Debt settlement ads lead with the amount you might save. The honest question is what's left after the costs. Some of those costs are fees you can calculate up front. Others, like late fees, lawsuits and taxes, depend on how the program goes. Here's how to add them up before you decide.

Cost 1: The settlement company's fee

Most debt settlement companies charge 15% to 25% of the total balance you enroll, according to CNBC Select. The fee is usually based on what you enrolled, not on what was saved, so it can be large even when the savings are modest.

Under the FTC's Telemarketing Sales Rule, companies that sell by phone can't collect that fee until they've actually settled a debt, and they can only charge part of it for each debt they settle (FTC).

Cost 2: The dedicated account

You save in a dedicated account run by a third party, and you may pay a fee to use it (CFPB). Ask for the monthly amount and multiply it by the number of months the program is expected to last.

Cost 3: Balances that grow while you save

The CFPB warns that settlement companies typically encourage you to stop paying your credit cards, and that missed payments bring late fees, penalty interest and other charges. Those charges increase the balance the company is negotiating against. The CFPB also notes that companies often can't settle every debt, and penalties on unsettled debts "may wipe out any savings" on the ones that do settle.

Cost 4: Your credit

The CFPB says debt settlement can hurt your credit scores and your ability to get credit in the future. If you expect to apply for a mortgage, a car loan or an apartment in the next few years, count that as a real cost.

Cost 5: Lawsuits

Working with a settlement company may lead a creditor to sue you to collect (CFPB). A lawsuit can bring court costs or wage garnishment, depending on your state. Ask any company what happens to you if a creditor sues during the program.

Cost 6: Taxes on forgiven debt

Canceled debt is generally taxable income, and creditors may send you Form 1099-C. There are exceptions, including debt canceled while you were insolvent (IRS Topic 431). If $8,000 of debt is forgiven, you may owe tax on $8,000 of income. A tax professional can tell you whether an exclusion applies.

A worked example

This is a hypothetical example to show the math. Your numbers will be different.

Item Amount
Credit card debt enrolled $25,000
Hypothetical settlements totaling 50% of balances $12,500 paid to creditors
Company fee at 20% of enrolled debt $5,000
Account fees ($10/month for 36 months) $360
Total paid $17,860
Debt forgiven $12,500 (may be taxable)
Apparent savings before taxes $7,140

Now adjust for the risks. If balances grow with late fees before they settle, if one creditor refuses to settle, or if you owe income tax on the forgiven $12,500, the savings shrink. In some cases they disappear.

When it might still be worth it

Settlement can make sense when:

  • You owe a large amount of unsecured debt, often $10,000 or more
  • You have a genuine hardship and can't keep up with minimum payments
  • A nonprofit debt management plan wouldn't be affordable
  • You're prepared for a credit score hit for several years
  • You can make steady monthly deposits for two to four years

It's usually not worth it if you can afford a debt management plan, if you qualify for a lower-rate consolidation loan, or if you need good credit soon.
