Can a Creditor Sue You While You're in a Debt Relief Program?

Yes, creditors can still sue during debt settlement or a repayment plan. Here's why it happens, what to do if you're served, and how to lower the risk.

October 20, 2026 · 3 min read

This article is educational and not financial advice. BizMasterPro may be compensated when you call providers we feature.

Short answer: yes. Enrolling in a debt relief program doesn't stop a creditor or debt collector from filing a lawsuit. The FTC warns that "only scammers will say they can stop all debt collection lawsuits" (FTC). Here's why lawsuits happen, what to do if you're served, and how to reduce the risk.

This is educational information, not legal advice. If you've been sued, talk to a lawyer or legal aid office.

Why lawsuits happen during debt relief

The risk depends on the kind of program.

  • Debt settlement. Most settlement programs have you pause payments to creditors while you save up money in a dedicated account. Missed payments lead to late fees, collection activity and sometimes lawsuits. Nothing requires a creditor to wait for a settlement offer.
  • Debt management plan. You keep making monthly payments through a credit counselor, so accounts generally stay current. Lawsuits are less likely as long as payments stay on schedule, but a missed or late plan payment can still lead to collection action.
  • Doing it yourself. If you negotiate directly and pause payments while you do, the same risks as settlement apply.
Program Are you paying creditors? Lawsuit risk
Debt settlement Usually not, while you save Higher
Debt management plan Yes, every month through the plan Lower, if payments stay on time
Bankruptcy Handled through the court Different rules apply; ask a bankruptcy attorney

Ask any company, in writing, what happens if a creditor sues while you're enrolled, and whether it offers any help.

What to do if you're sued

1. Don't ignore it

The CFPB says to respond "by the date specified in the court papers," either yourself or through a lawyer (CFPB). Responding doesn't mean you agree you owe the debt. It means the collector has to prove its case.

2. Understand what happens if you don't respond

If you don't respond, the court could issue a "default judgment" for the amount claimed plus allowed fees, interest and attorney costs. Depending on state law, the creditor may then be able to garnish wages, place a lien on property or freeze funds in a bank account (CFPB). A judgment can be hard to undo afterward.

3. Know which income is protected

Many federal benefits are generally exempt from court-ordered garnishment, including Social Security, SSI, veterans benefits and federal student aid, with exceptions for things like unpaid taxes, child support and student loans (FTC). States have their own rules too.

4. Get legal help

An attorney who handles debt collection cases can explain your federal and state protections. Some offer free or reduced-fee help, and local legal aid offices may help for free if you qualify (CFPB). The FTC points to LawHelp.org as a place to start.

5. Ask about settling before judgment

The CFPB notes you may be able to negotiate a compromise or settlement before the court rules. If you do, get it in writing. The FTC says to get a signed letter stating that your payment settles the entire debt, and to keep records of every payment (FTC).

How to lower the risk

  1. Get your full financial picture reviewed first. A nonprofit credit counselor through the NFCC can explain options that keep payments current.
  2. Understand the trade-offs before you pause payments. Settlement can bring credit damage, lawsuits, fees and taxes on forgiven debt, and programs often take 2–4 years.
  3. Read every agreement. Know which debts are enrolled and what the company does if you're sued.
  4. Open all mail from creditors and courts. Missing a court deadline is the costliest mistake.
  5. Check old debts. If a debt may be past your state's statute of limitations, the FTC says a collector can't legally sue over it, and you should raise that in court if you're sued. Making a payment can sometimes restart the clock, so get advice first.
