How Debt Settlement Works, Step by Step

What actually happens from sign-up to the last settled card: the savings account, the negotiations, when fees are allowed, and the risks along the way.

October 8, 2026 · 4 min read

This article is educational and not financial advice. BizMasterPro may be compensated when you call providers we feature.

Debt settlement is a deal in which a creditor agrees to accept less than the full balance to close out a debt. Companies that offer settlement programs usually work with people who have large credit card balances they can't realistically pay in full. The process takes years, has real costs, and doesn't work for everyone. Here's what it looks like from start to finish, based on guidance from the Federal Trade Commission and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau.

Step 1: The company reviews your debts and discloses the terms

You list your unsecured debts, usually credit cards, and the company decides which ones it can enroll. Before you sign up, a company selling its program by phone must clearly tell you, according to the FTC's Telemarketing Sales Rule guidance:

  • All of its fees and any conditions or restrictions
  • How long it expects the program to take
  • How much you'll need to save before it makes a settlement offer to each creditor
  • What can happen if you stop making payments to your creditors

If a company won't put these in writing, treat that as a reason to walk away.

Step 2: You save money in a dedicated account

Instead of paying your creditors each month, you deposit a set amount into a dedicated savings account. The FTC says this account must be held at an insured financial institution, and the money in it, including any interest, belongs to you. You can withdraw it at any time. If you leave the program, the funds must be returned within seven business days, minus any fees the company has legitimately earned (FTC).

The account manager may charge a reasonable monthly fee to run the account (FTC).

Step 3: Your accounts usually fall behind

This is the step most ads skip. The CFPB says settlement companies typically encourage you to stop paying your credit card bills, both to build up the savings and to push creditors to negotiate. While you aren't paying:

  • Late fees and penalty interest are added each month, so your balances grow.
  • Your credit score is likely to drop, often significantly.
  • Collectors may call, and creditors can sue you.

Step 4: The company negotiates once you've saved enough

When your account holds enough to make a credible offer on one debt, the company contacts that creditor. The FTC gives an example: if you owe $10,000 and the company's data suggests the creditor will settle for about $6,000, it must tell you up front that you'll need to save about $6,000 for that debt.

Creditors don't have to negotiate. Some refuse to work with settlement companies at all, and many programs can't settle every enrolled debt (CFPB).

Step 5: You approve the deal and the creditor is paid

You have to agree to each settlement, and the creditor's agreement must be in writing. Then at least one payment goes to the creditor from your account under the new terms. Only after all of that is the company allowed to charge a fee for that debt (FTC).

Step 6: The company is paid, one settled debt at a time

A debt settlement company can't collect fees before it settles a debt. Each time it settles one, it may charge only a portion of its total fee. The FTC describes two common ways to set fees: a share of the debt that was resolved, or a percentage of the amount you saved. Ask which method a company uses and get an example in dollars.

Step 7: Repeat until the program ends

The cycle of saving, negotiating, approving and paying repeats for each enrolled debt. Programs commonly run two to four years. Debts that never settle keep growing with fees and interest, which can wipe out the savings on the debts that did settle (CFPB).

After the program: taxes

Forgiven debt is generally treated as taxable income in the year it's canceled, and creditors may send you Form 1099-C. There are exceptions, including debt canceled while you were insolvent, which is reported on Form 982 (IRS Topic 431). Talk to a tax professional before you file.

Is there another way?

If your income covers your minimum payments but the interest is the problem, a nonprofit credit counselor may be able to set up a debt management plan with lower rates and no missed payments. You can find one through the NFCC. The FTC also notes you can try negotiating a settlement directly with a creditor yourself. If you reach a deal, get it in writing.
