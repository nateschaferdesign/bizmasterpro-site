Do You Pay Taxes on Settled Debt? Form 1099-C Explained

Why forgiven credit card debt is usually taxable, what a 1099-C shows, and how the insolvency exclusion can reduce or erase the tax.

October 14, 2026 · 3 min read

This article is educational and not financial advice. BizMasterPro may be compensated when you call providers we feature.

When a creditor agrees to accept less than you owe, the part it forgives doesn't simply disappear. In most cases, the IRS treats it as income. The good news is that there are exclusions, and many people in financial trouble qualify for one. Here's how it works, based on IRS Publication 4681, the IRS guide to canceled debts.

This is general information, not tax advice. A tax professional can tell you how the rules apply to you.

The basic rule: canceled debt is income

If a debt is forgiven for less than the full amount, the unpaid part counts as canceled debt. Generally, canceled debt is ordinary income unless an exception or exclusion applies (IRS Pub. 4681).

For example, if you owed $10,000 on a credit card and settled it for $4,000, the $6,000 that was forgiven may count as income on your tax return for that year.

What Form 1099-C is

Form 1099-C, Cancellation of Debt, is the form a lender sends when it cancels a debt. The IRS says lenders such as financial institutions must file one and send you a copy when the canceled amount is $600 or more (IRS Pub. 4681).

The boxes most people need to understand:

Box What it shows
Box 2 The amount of debt canceled. It may include principal plus interest and fees.
Box 3 Any interest included in the Box 2 amount
Box 6 A letter code for why the form was filed. Code F means "by agreement," which is common for settlements.

If anything on the form is wrong, contact the creditor (IRS Topic 431).

No 1099-C? You may still owe

This catches people off guard. The IRS says that even if you didn't receive a Form 1099-C, you must report canceled debt as income unless an exception or exclusion applies (IRS Pub. 4681). For nonbusiness debt like credit cards, taxable canceled debt is generally reported on Schedule 1 of Form 1040.

The insolvency exclusion

For canceled credit card debt, the IRS names two main exclusions: bankruptcy and insolvency (IRS Pub. 4681).

You were insolvent if, immediately before the debt was canceled, your total liabilities were more than the fair market value of everything you owned. You can exclude canceled debt only up to the amount you were insolvent.

A simple example

This is a hypothetical example.

Item Amount
Total debts right before cancellation $48,000
Fair market value of everything you own $40,000
Amount of insolvency $8,000
Debt canceled in the settlement $6,000
Amount you can exclude $6,000 (the smaller of the two)
Taxable canceled debt $0

If the canceled debt had been $10,000 instead, you could exclude $8,000, and $2,000 would generally be taxable unless another exclusion applied.

What counts as assets and debts

The IRS insolvency worksheet in Publication 4681 lists what to include. Assets include cash, cars, household goods, and also retirement accounts and pension interests, which people often forget to count. Debts include credit cards, mortgages, car loans, medical bills, student loans, past-due taxes and other amounts you owe.

How to claim it

To claim the insolvency exclusion, attach Form 982 to your federal tax return, check the box on line 1b, and enter the excluded amount on line 2 (IRS Pub. 4681). Form 982 also asks you to reduce certain "tax attributes," which is one reason it's worth getting professional help.

The bankruptcy exclusion

Debt canceled in a Title 11 bankruptcy case isn't included in income. It's claimed on Form 982 too, using line 1a.

How to plan ahead

  1. Keep your settlement letters. They show the original balance and what you paid.
  2. Estimate your insolvency on the date each debt settles, not just at the start of the program.
  3. Watch for 1099-C forms in January and February after the year a debt settles.
  4. Set money aside if you don't expect to qualify for an exclusion.
  5. Talk to a tax professional before you file, especially if you settled several debts.
