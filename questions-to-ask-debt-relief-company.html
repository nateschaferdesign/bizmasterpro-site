12 Questions to Ask a Debt Relief Company Before You Sign

The questions to ask any debt relief company about fees, timelines, your savings account and risks, plus what federal rules say they must tell you before you enroll.

November 8, 2026 · 3 min read

This article is educational and not financial advice. BizMasterPro may be compensated when you call providers we feature.

Before you enroll in any debt relief program, you have the right to clear answers. Under the FTC's Telemarketing Sales Rule, companies that sell debt relief by phone must disclose certain information "truthfully and clearly and conspicuously" before you sign up (FTC). The questions below help you get those answers, and a few more.

This is educational information, not financial or legal advice.

About fees

1. What are all your fees, in dollars? Companies must disclose all fees before you sign up. If the fee is a percentage of what you'd save, they must tell you both the percentage and the estimated dollar amount for you (FTC).

2. When do you collect your fee? The safe answer: only after a debt is settled. Under the rule, a company can't charge a fee until it has settled or changed at least one of your debts, you've agreed to the result, and you've made at least one payment to that creditor. If anyone wants money first, walk away. See our guide to debt relief scams.

3. How is the fee split across my debts? Fees for each settled debt must either be proportional to that debt's share of your total, or the same percentage of savings for every debt (FTC).

4. Are there other costs, like account fees? Ask about monthly fees for the dedicated savings account, which may be charged by a separate account provider.

About timing

5. How long until you make an offer to each of my creditors? Companies must give a good-faith estimate of how many months or years you'll wait before they make an offer likely to settle each debt.

6. How much do I need to save before each offer? They must also tell you how much money, or what percentage of each debt, you'll need to save first.

7. How long will the whole program take? Settlement programs often take 2–4 years. Make sure your budget can support steady deposits for that long.

About your money

8. Who owns the money in my savings account? You do. The company must tell you that you own the funds, can leave at any time without penalty, and will get back everything in the account except fees legitimately earned. If you leave, the money must be returned within seven business days (FTC).

9. What happens if I need to pause or leave? Get the cancellation and refund terms in writing. If the company doesn't give refunds, it must tell you before you sign up.

About risks

10. What will happen to my credit and my accounts? If a program relies on you pausing payments to creditors, the company must explain the possible consequences: damage to your credit report and score, creditors suing you or continuing collections, and new fees and interest that increase what you owe (FTC). Our guide on being sued during debt relief covers what to do if that happens.

11. What happens if a creditor won't settle? Not every creditor agrees to settle. Ask what percentage of debts the company typically settles and what happens to the rest.

12. Could I owe taxes on forgiven debt? Forgiven debt can count as taxable income. A company should explain this, and a tax professional can tell you whether an exclusion like insolvency applies. See our Form 1099-C guide.

Red flags in the answers

Walk away if the company:

  • Asks for fees before settling anything
  • Promises to settle every debt or stop all lawsuits
  • Won't put fees and timelines in writing
  • Enrolls you without reviewing your finances
  • Says the program comes from the government

Do your homework

  • Check the company with your state attorney general and local consumer protection agency, and search its name with "complaints."
  • Read the full agreement before signing, including the fee schedule and cancellation terms.
  • Compare alternatives. A nonprofit credit counselor through the NFCC can explain options like a debt management plan, which repays your balance in full, often with lower interest.

Debt settlement can help some people, but it comes with real trade-offs: credit damage, possible lawsuits, fees, possible taxes and a timeline of 2–4 years. Asking these questions upfront helps you decide with clear eyes.
