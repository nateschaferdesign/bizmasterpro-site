What CAN-SPAM Requires From Small Senders

A plain-English checklist of the CAN-SPAM Act's eight requirements for small businesses, creators and affiliate marketers, including the postal address, unsubscribe rules and penalties.

November 17, 2026 · 4 min read

Disclosure: BizMasterPro is reader-supported. Some links are affiliate links, and we may earn a commission if you sign up for a paid plan, at no extra cost to you.

Short answer: if you send marketing email in the U.S., the CAN-SPAM Act applies to you, no matter how small your list is. Each marketing email needs honest sender details, an honest subject line, a valid physical postal address and a working way to opt out, and you have to honor opt-outs within 10 business days. According to the FTC's CAN-SPAM compliance guide, each separate email that breaks the rules can bring penalties of up to $53,088.

This guide walks through all eight requirements in plain English, with what they mean for solo creators and affiliate marketers. It's general information, not legal advice.

Does CAN-SPAM apply to small senders?

Yes. CAN-SPAM has no minimum list size. It covers commercial email, which the FTC describes as messages whose primary purpose is advertising or promoting a commercial product or service. It doesn't matter whether you send to 50 people or 50,000.

What matters is the message's primary purpose:

  • Commercial: promotes a product, service or commercial website. A weekly newsletter that recommends tools with affiliate links usually counts. Full CAN-SPAM rules apply.
  • Transactional or relationship: for example a receipt, account notice or course access details. These are exempt from most requirements, but they still can't contain false or misleading routing information.
  • Mixed: if a reader would think from the subject line that the email is a promotion, or the transactional part doesn't appear mainly at the beginning, the FTC treats it as commercial.

The eight requirements, in plain English

1. Don't use false or misleading header information

The "From," "To," "Reply-To" and routing details must be accurate and identify who sent the message. Send from a real address on your own domain, use your real business or brand name, and don't pose as someone else.

2. Don't use deceptive subject lines

The subject line must reflect what's in the email. "Your order has shipped" on a promotional email is out. So is "Re:" on a first message to make it look like a reply.

3. Identify the message as an ad

The FTC says you must disclose clearly and conspicuously that the message is an advertisement. There's no single required wording. Many small senders handle this by making the promotional nature obvious in the content and including a short note near the footer.

4. Include a valid physical postal address

Every marketing email needs one of these:

  • a current street address,
  • a post office box registered with the U.S. Postal Service, or
  • a private mailbox registered with a commercial mail receiving agency.

If you work from home and don't want your home address in every email, a P.O. box or a registered private mailbox is the usual choice. Most email platforms let you set the address once, and it appears in every footer.

5. Tell people how to opt out

Each email must explain clearly how to stop receiving marketing messages, using a return email address or another simple online method such as an unsubscribe link. You can offer choices, such as weekly only or deals only, but there must be an option to stop all marketing email. Make sure your spam filter doesn't block opt-out replies.

6. Subscribers and members can opt out too

Joining a membership, buying a course or creating an account doesn't remove someone's right to opt out of marketing email. If you send a message without an unsubscribe link, its primary purpose must fit one of the Act's transactional or relationship categories.

7. Honor opt-outs promptly

The FTC's rules here are specific:

  • Your opt-out method must keep working for at least 30 days after you send the message.
  • You must honor a request within 10 business days.
  • You can't charge a fee, ask for more than an email address, or require any step beyond replying to an email or visiting one web page.
  • Once someone opts out, you can't sell or transfer their address, except to a company you've hired to help you comply.

8. Monitor what others do on your behalf

You can't contract away responsibility. If you hire a freelancer or agency to send your email, the FTC says both the company whose product is promoted and the company that sends the message can be held legally responsible.

What this means for affiliate marketers

If you promote other companies' products by email, you're sending commercial email, so CAN-SPAM applies to every promotional issue. A few extra habits help:

  • Disclose affiliate links. This comes from the FTC's endorsement rules rather than CAN-SPAM. The FTC's Endorsement Guides FAQ explains that readers should know when you earn a commission.
  • Don't use a vendor's swipe copy blindly. If a supplied email has a misleading subject line, you're still responsible for sending it.
  • Never buy or rent lists. CAN-SPAM doesn't require advance permission, but purchased lists hurt deliverability, and most email platforms ban them in their terms.

A quick pre-send checklist

Before every marketing email, check:

Check Done?
"From" name and address are accurate and on your domain
Subject line matches the content
It's clear the email is promotional
Valid postal address appears in the footer
Unsubscribe link works and is easy to find
Affiliate links are disclosed
Opt-out requests from the last send have been processed

How your email platform helps

Most email marketing platforms handle the mechanical parts for you. They add an unsubscribe link and your postal address to every footer, and they remove unsubscribed contacts automatically. That covers requirements 4, 5 and 7 as long as you enter a valid address in your settings and don't remove the footer.

The parts no software can handle are honest subject lines, accurate sender details and clear disclosures. Those are up to you.

The bottom line

CAN-SPAM isn't complicated for small senders: be honest about who you are and what you're sending, include a real postal address, and make opting out easy and fast. Set up your footer once, use the checklist before every send, and you've covered the basics.

If you're choosing a platform, look for one that adds compliant footers and handles unsubscribes automatically. All-in-one tools like systeme.io include this on their free plan.
